# Training, Nutrition, Sleep and Recovery Science for MINMAX Quests

_Research report for MINMAX · 2026-10-02 · status: draft, independently fact-checked afterwards_

## Summary for the founder

- **The minimum effective dose is small, so Tier I quests can be easy and still honest.** The 2025 Pelland meta-regression (67 studies, 2,058 participants) finds that about **4 "fractional" sets per muscle per week** is enough for detectable hypertrophy and about **1–2 sets per week** for detectable strength gains. Strength gains level off at roughly 5 sets per week, while hypertrophy keeps rising with diminishing returns [1]. A beginner quest of "2 sessions, a few hard sets per muscle" is backed by evidence. (confidence: high)
- **Guidelines give you Tier A anchors for free.** WHO 2020 recommends 150–300 min of moderate (or 75–150 min of vigorous) aerobic activity per week, muscle strengthening on ≥2 days, and multicomponent balance and strength work on ≥3 days for people 65+. It also says "some physical activity is better than none" [3]. Use these as the Tier A quests in every region. (confidence: high)
- **Zone 2 is marketing-inflated.** A 2025 Sports Medicine review concludes that evidence "does not support Zone 2 training as the optimal intensity" for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation gains, and that the general public should not give up higher intensities for it [10]. Wearables mostly define Zone 2 as 60–70 % of an *estimated* max HR (220 − age by default on Garmin) [11]. MINMAX should call it "easy aerobic" and claim nothing about mitochondria. (confidence: high)
- **For recreational athletes, pyramidal is at least as good as polarized.** An individual-participant network meta-analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no overall difference. Recreational athletes may improve VO2max more with a pyramidal distribution [9]. Do not sell "80/20 polarized" as a rule. (confidence: medium)
- **Mobility quests should be cheap: about 10 min of static stretching per week.** A 2024 meta-regression (189 studies, 6,654 adults) found no extra flexibility gain beyond 4 min per session or 10 min per week [18]. Strength training through full range of motion gives similar ROM gains to stretching [20]. Stretching does not prevent injuries; strength training does (Lauersen: injuries cut to less than one third) [19][21]. (confidence: high)
- **Movement: 7,000 steps is the new honest default, not 10,000.** Compared with 2,000 steps per day, about 7,000 steps per day is associated with 47 % lower all-cause mortality, and gains beyond 7,000 are modest [27]. Paluch found mortality risk levelling off at 6,000–8,000 steps for adults 60+ and at 8,000–10,000 for younger adults [26]. VILPA (1–2-minute vigorous bursts in daily life) is a strong observational signal and suits "exercise snack" quests [29][30]. (confidence: high for steps, medium for VILPA causality)
- **Nutrition quests should add things, not restrict them.** Evidence-backed additive targets: fibre 25–29 g/day [33], protein up to about 1.6 g/kg/day for people who lift [8], and more cooking from minimally processed ingredients [35]. Alcohol guidance tightened between 2023 and 2026: WHO says "no safe level" [36], and the German DGE (2024) says "am besten null Promille" [37]. In January 2026 the US guidelines dropped numeric limits and now just say "consume less" [38]. The "2 litres of water" target lacks evidence [39], and time-restricted eating gave no extra weight loss over calorie restriction alone in a 12-month RCT [40]. (confidence: high)
- **Supplements: only creatine has strong general-population support.** Creatine at 3–5 g/day is effective and safe [41]. For vitamin D, the Endocrine Society 2024 advises against routine testing and against doses above the RDA for healthy adults under 75 [42]. Omega-3 at 1 g/day did not reduce major CV events or cancer in VITAL [43]. Everything else in the "Top 0.1 %" stack sits at Tier D or below. (confidence: high)
- **Recovery: put sleep regularity first and HRV second.** Regularity predicted mortality better than duration in about 61k UK Biobank participants [45]. Evening alcohol reduces HRV-based recovery in a dose-dependent way [46]. HRV-guided training beats fixed plans only slightly (VO2max SMD 0.13, not significant) [50]. Cold-water immersion right after lifting probably blunts hypertrophy [48]. The sauna evidence is observational and comes from Finnish men [47]. (confidence: high/medium)
- **Safety rails: avoid pseudo-precise load ratios.** The 10 % rule did not reduce injuries in a 532-runner RCT [52], and the acute:chronic workload ratio has "no evidence" supporting its use [53]. MINMAX should use simple, transparent rails instead: stepwise tiers, one "load" quest per stat per week, gated Arena access, and no penalty for missed weeks.

## Findings

### 1. Strength (The Forge)

**Volume dose-response.** Pelland et al. (Sports Medicine, accepted Oct 2025, preprint 2024) meta-regressed 67 studies with 2,058 participants (79 % male, mean age 25) [1]. Indirect sets counted as 0.5 ("fractional") predicted outcomes best. The posterior probability that more volume increases both hypertrophy and strength was 100 %, with diminishing returns that were "considerably more pronounced" for strength [1]. The efficiency tables are directly usable for quest tiers:
- **Hypertrophy:** a minimum effective dose of 4 fractional sets per week; 5–10 sets is "higher efficiency"; 11–18 is intermediate; 19–29 is lower; 30–42 is lowest; 43+ is unclear [1].
- **Strength:** a minimum effective dose of 1 set per week; 2 sets is higher efficiency; 3–4 is intermediate; beyond 5 sets, "additional weekly sets do not consistently enhance strength gains" [1].

Earlier, Schoenfeld 2017 reported non-significantly larger effects for ≥9 vs <9 weekly sets (ES 0.46 vs 0.32), as summarised in [1]. (confidence: high)

**Frequency.** At equal volume, frequency had a negligible effect on hypertrophy. Strength rose with frequency (fractional frequency 1 → 2: estimated gain 12.7 % → 17.3 %), and returns accelerated downward beyond that [1]. Practical rule: spread sets over 2+ sessions, mainly for strength and for practising the skill. (confidence: high)

**Proximity to failure.** Robinson et al. 2024 (Sports Medicine) estimated repetitions in reserve (RIR) across studies. Hypertrophy improved as sets got closer to failure, while strength was largely unaffected [2]. Quest copy should say "end most sets with 1–3 reps left in the tank" and should not require training to failure. (confidence: medium; RIR was estimated from study descriptions)

**Guideline and mortality anchors.** WHO requires muscle strengthening on ≥2 days per week for all adults [3]. A meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies (Momma 2022, BJSM) found 10–17 % lower all-cause, CVD, cancer and diabetes risk with muscle-strengthening activity. The association was J-shaped, with the largest reduction at about 30–60 min per week. Combining strength and aerobic activity was associated with 40 % lower all-cause mortality [4]. (confidence: medium, observational)

**Beginner vs intermediate.** The volume tables imply that beginners get most of the benefit from the first sets, so Tier I = minimum effective dose. Later tiers add sets with diminishing returns. Pelland adjusted for training status but did not supply separate beginner curves, so specific progression schemes (linear vs double progression) are **unverified** as superior. (confidence: medium)

**Deloads.** Evidence is thin. A Delphi consensus of coaches describes deloads as planned reductions in volume or intensity used to manage fatigue [7]. In a 9-week RCT, a one-week *complete* training break mid-programme led to slightly smaller lower-body strength gains than continuous training, with no difference in hypertrophy [6]. MINMAX should offer an optional "lighter week" and should not prescribe one. (confidence: medium)

**Load ranges and bodyweight.** Pelland and Robinson did not find load to dominate outcomes once effort is high. Bodyweight progressions (push-ups, split squats, rows with household items) are therefore legitimate Forge quests as long as sets end near failure. Inference from [1][2]. (confidence: medium)

**40+ considerations.** WHO adds multicomponent balance and strength work on ≥3 days per week for 65+ to prevent falls [3]. Muscle power declines "earlier and more precipitously" than strength with age [24]. In LIFTMOR, 8 months of twice-weekly supervised 5×5 at >85 % 1RM plus impact training improved bone indices and function in postmenopausal women with low bone mass, with minimal adverse events [5]. Heavy-load bone quests are therefore evidence-based but must be gated and framed as supervised. Protein supplementation works less well with increasing age [8].

### 2. Aerobic (The Engine)

**Dose.** WHO: 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous per week, with more giving additional benefit [3].

**CRF and mortality.** Mandsager 2018 (122,007 treadmill-tested adults) found elite vs low fitness associated with an adjusted HR of 0.20 for all-cause mortality and no upper limit of benefit [15]. Kokkinos 2022 (750,302 US veterans aged 30–95) found HR 0.86 per +1 MET, the lowest risk at about 14 METs, and roughly 4× the risk for the least fit (20th percentile) vs the extremely fit (98th) [16]. These are associations. They justify VO2max as a headline stat but not causal promises in quest copy. (confidence: high for association)

**Intensity distribution.** In Rosenblat 2025 (IPD network meta-analysis, 13 studies, 348 athletes), polarized vs pyramidal showed SMD −0.06 for VO2max and −0.05 for time trial, both non-significant. Recreational athletes may do better with pyramidal [9]. The participants were already trained (VO2max 46.6–68.3), so the findings do not transfer directly to beginners. (confidence: medium)

**Zone 2.** Storoschuk, Gibala and Gurd (Sports Medicine 2025) define Zone 2 as intensity just below the first lactate threshold. They found no substantive evidence that it beats higher intensities for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation capacity, and they note it may fall *below* guideline moderate-to-vigorous intensity [10]. Garmin's default Zone 2 is 60–70 % of max HR, with max HR estimated as 220 − age until workouts refine it [11]. A wearable's "Zone 2 minutes" is therefore a percentage-of-estimated-HRmax band, not a lactate measurement. (confidence: high)

**VO2max protocols.**
- *Milanović 2015* (Sports Medicine): endurance training improved VO2max by about 4.9 ml/kg/min vs controls, and HIIT produced larger gains than continuous training in healthy young to middle-aged adults [13]. (confidence: high)
- *Helgerud 2007* (MSSE): 4×4 min at 90–95 % HRmax, 3× per week for 8 weeks, improved VO2max more than work-matched moderate or lactate-threshold training. The magnitude reported in secondary sources (about 7 %) should be checked against the full text [12]. (confidence: medium)
- *Billat 30/30*: 30 s at vVO2max alternating with 30 s at 50 % vVO2max kept trained runners at VO2max longer than continuous runs. The training-effect figures circulating (about 10 % in 8–10 weeks) come from secondary sources and are **unverified** [14]. (confidence: low–medium)

### 3. Mobility (Temple of Motion)

**What stretching changes.** Thomas 2018 found that weekly time (≥5 min per week, ideally on ≥5 days) predicts ROM gains, while time per session does not [17]. The 2024 Sports Medicine meta-regression (189 studies) found chronic static stretching has a large effect (g = 0.96). Gains plateau at about 10 min per week and 4 min per session, are not moderated by intensity, age, sex or frequency, and are larger in people with poor baseline flexibility [18]. (confidence: high)

**Static vs dynamic.** Behm 2016: immediately after stretching, static stretching changed performance by −3.7 %, dynamic by +1.3 % and PNF by −4.4 %. Static holds under 60 s per muscle cost only −1.1 %. ROM effects usually lasted under 30 minutes, and static/PNF stretching had no clear effect on injuries [19]. Recommendation: short static holds inside a warm-up that ends with dynamic activity [19]. (confidence: high)

**Strength as mobility.** Afonso 2021 (11 RCTs, 452 participants) found no difference between strength training and stretching for ROM (ES −0.22, p = 0.21) [20]. Full-ROM lifting can count toward Temple quests. (confidence: medium)

**Injury prevention.** Lauersen 2014: strength training reduced sports injuries to under one third and almost halved overuse injuries, while stretching showed no significant effect [21]. Lauersen 2018: the injury reduction appears dose-dependent with strength-training volume and intensity [22]. (confidence: high)

**Balance in older adults.** Cochrane (Sherrington 2019; 108 RCTs, 23,407 participants) found exercise reduces the rate of falls by 23%, with balance and functional exercise as core components [23]. WHO makes this a ≥3 days per week recommendation for 65+ [3]. (confidence: high)

**Home measurement.** Sit-and-reach, knee-to-wall and single-leg stance are low-cost and already in `metric.ts`. Validity and norms are covered in normative-data.md and not repeated here.

### 4. Power (The Arena)

Power declines earlier and faster than strength with age and predicts functional limitations [24]. A systematic review of lower-limb plyometrics in older adults found improvements in strength, jump height and functional tests (sit-to-stand, TUG, stair climb). No study reported more injuries, but the review recommends supervision and caution with unilateral drills (calf strains) [25]. For recreational adults, the logical progression is: (1) strength base, (2) "fast intent" concentric lifting, (3) low-amplitude bilateral hops, (4) jumps and short hill sprints. The progression order and contact counts are **design choices, not tested protocols**. (confidence: medium)

### 5. Movement (The Wilds)

- **Steps.** Paluch 2022 (15 cohorts) found mortality benefit levelling at 6,000–8,000 steps per day for 60+ and 8,000–10,000 for under-60s [26]. Ding 2025 (Lancet Public Health, 57 studies) compared 7,000 with 2,000 steps per day and found 47 % lower mortality, 38 % lower dementia risk and 27 % lower type 2 diabetes risk. Going from 2,000 to 4,000 already helps, and gains beyond 7,000 are "modest" [27]. (confidence: high, observational)
- **Sedentary breaks.** Dunstan 2012: 2 minutes of light or moderate walking every 20 minutes over 5 hours reduced post-meal glucose and insulin AUC vs uninterrupted sitting in overweight or obese adults [28]. WHO: "replacing sedentary time with physical activity of any intensity... provides health benefits", but no threshold could be quantified [3]. (confidence: high acute, low long-term)
- **VILPA / exercise snacks.** Stamatakis 2022 (Nature Medicine; 25,241 non-exercisers, UK Biobank) found 3 bouts per day of 1–2 minutes associated with 38–40 % lower all-cause and cancer mortality and 48–49 % lower CVD mortality [29]. A follow-up found stronger MACE associations in women (median 3.4 min/day: HR 0.55) than in men [30]. These are observational wrist-accelerometer data with possible reverse causation. (confidence: medium)
- **Green exercise.** Barton & Pretty 2010 (10 UK studies, 1,252 people) found improved mood (d = 0.54) and self-esteem (d = 0.46). The largest self-esteem effect came at a 5-minute dose [31]. (confidence: medium; short-term self-report outcomes)
- **NEAT.** Non-exercise activity is real, but nothing in this pass was verified at a level that justifies a separate NEAT quest. Steps, breaks and VILPA cover it.

### 6. Nutrition (The Garden)

- **Protein total.** Morton 2018 (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants) found protein supplementation beyond about 1.62 g/kg/day total added no further fat-free mass gains during resistance training. The upper CI reaches about 2.2 g/kg [8]. (confidence: high)
- **Protein distribution.** Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 suggest about 0.4 g/kg per meal across ≥4 meals for maximal anabolism [32]. This is a narrative/mechanistic inference, so tier C or D. (confidence: medium)
- **Fibre.** Reynolds 2019 (Lancet; about 135 million person-years of prospective data plus 58 trials with 4,635 adults) found the largest risk reductions at 25–29 g/day, with 15–30 % lower mortality and lower CHD, stroke, T2D and colorectal cancer risk when the highest fibre intakes were compared with the lowest [33]. The "25–38 g" in the brief combines this with national adequate-intake values that were not re-verified here.
- **Dietary pattern.** PREDIMED was retracted and republished in 2018 after randomisation problems affecting 1,588 of 7,447 participants. Its result, about 30 % fewer major CV events with a Mediterranean diet plus olive oil or nuts, held up [34]. (confidence: medium–high)
- **Ultra-processed food.** Hall 2019 (inpatient crossover, n = 20, 2 weeks per diet) found that an ultra-processed diet led people to eat 508 ± 106 kcal/day more and gain weight, even though the diets presented were matched for nutrients [35]. This is strong causal evidence for energy intake, but the trial was small and short. (confidence: medium)
- **Alcohol, 2023–2026.** WHO/Europe (Lancet Public Health, Jan 2023): "the risk... starts from the first drop". Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen, and half of alcohol-attributable cancers in the region come from light or moderate drinking [36]. DGE (Aug 2024): no risk-free amount, so best to abstain. For healthy adults who do drink, 1–2 standard drinks per week (under 27 g) is considered low-risk [37]. The 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines (7 Jan 2026) removed the 1-drink/2-drink limits and now say "consume less alcohol for better overall health" [38]. (confidence: high)
- **Hydration.** Yamada 2022 (Science; doubly labelled water, 5,604 people, 26 countries) found water turnover of about 4.3 L/day in men and 3.4 L/day in women, varying widely with size, activity and climate. The authors say the 8×8-oz rule "is not backed up by objective evidence" [39]. MINMAX should have no water-intake quest. (confidence: high)
- **Time-restricted eating.** Liu 2022 (NEJM, n = 139, 12 months): 8-hour TRE plus calorie restriction led to −8.0 kg vs −6.3 kg for calorie restriction alone, a non-significant difference [40]. TRE is a legitimate *tool for adherence*, not a metabolic advantage. (confidence: high)
- **Supplements.**
  - Creatine: 3–5 g/day maintains stores and is safe long term in healthy people. The only consistent side effect is weight gain [41]. (Tier B)
  - Vitamin D: the Endocrine Society (2024) advises against routine 25(OH)D testing and against doses above the RDA for healthy adults under 75. Higher doses are suggested for 75+, pregnancy, prediabetes and children [42]. (Tier A for "don't megadose")
  - Omega-3: 1 g/day in VITAL did not reduce major CV events or invasive cancer. A possible myocardial-infarction signal appeared in a secondary analysis [43]. (Tier C for general use)
  - NAC before training and 3–10 mg melatonin as general advice found no support in anything reviewed here. Treat them as Tier D or "not recommended" (no source found that supports them for the general population; **unverified** in the opposite direction too).

### 7. Recovery (The Sanctum)

- **Sleep extension.** Mah 2011 (11 Stanford basketball players) found sleep extension with ≥10 h in bed added about 111 min/night of sleep. Sprint time improved from 16.2 s to 15.5 s, and free-throw and 3-point accuracy rose about 9 % [44]. This was a small, uncontrolled, highly motivated sample. (confidence: low–medium for generalisation)
- **Regularity.** Windred et al. (Sleep 2024, about 61k UK Biobank participants with accelerometers) found the Sleep Regularity Index predicted mortality better than sleep duration [45]. Secondary sources cite SRI < 70 as higher risk and > 80 as a target. Those cut-offs are **unverified** against the primary paper. (confidence: medium for the direction, low for cut-offs)
- **Alcohol and sleep.** Pietilä 2018 (real-world Finnish employees) found alcohol reduced HRV-based recovery in the first 3 hours of sleep by 9.3, 24.0 and 39.2 percentage points for low, moderate and high intake. Physical activity did not protect against this [46]. This links naturally to wearable HRV. (confidence: medium–high)
- **Sauna.** Laukkanen 2015 (2,315 Finnish men aged 42–60, about 20-year follow-up): compared with 1 session per week, 4–7 sessions per week were associated with 63 % lower sudden cardiac death and 40 % lower all-cause mortality [47]. The data are observational, from men, in a culture where sauna is normal. (confidence: medium)
- **Cold exposure.** Piñero 2024 (8 studies) found that cold-water immersion right after resistance training "may attenuate hypertrophic changes", with at least a small reduction [48]. MINMAX should never pair a cold quest with a Forge day. (confidence: medium)
- **HRV-guided training.** Javaloyes 2019 (17 trained cyclists) found likely benefits in 40-min time-trial and peak power vs traditional periodisation [49]. The 2021 meta-analysis (8 studies, 199 participants) found VO2max SMD 0.13 (NS), endurance performance 0.20 (NS) and vagal HRV 0.50 (significant). Any superiority is "only by a small margin" [50]. Düking 2021 (JSAMS) found trivial-to-small effects on VO2max and performance and a medium effect on submaximal parameters [51]. HRV-guided training works as an *adaptive difficulty* feature. It should not be sold as a performance multiplier. (confidence: medium)
- **Rest days.** No dedicated evidence was verified in this pass. The Coleman deload RCT [6] suggests that short complete breaks cost little hypertrophy. Rest-day quests are justified as adherence and recovery design (Tier D/C).

### 8. Periodization and safety rails

- **10 % rule.** In the GRONORUN RCT (532 novice runners), a graded 13-week programme following the 10 % rule produced the same injury incidence as a standard 8-week programme (20.8 % vs 20.3 %) [52]. Keep weekly volume increases gradual as common sense, but do not label them evidence-based. (confidence: high)
- **ACWR.** Impellizzeri et al. 2020 found "no evidence supporting the use of ACWR" for reducing injury risk and described its statistical properties as making it "an inaccurate metric" [53]. Do not build a "load ratio" stat. (confidence: high)
- **Design rails derived from the above (not directly tested):** start every new user at Tier I for 2 weeks; step up a tier only after a fully completed week (already in `pickDifficulty`); drop a tier after a week under 50 %; allow one "load" quest per stat per week; gate the Arena behind strength and mobility; never schedule restrictive-nutrition quests automatically; and keep cold and Forge on separate days.

## Implications for MINMAX

1. **Rewrite the provisional templates.** The 18 entries in `packages/core/src/quests/templates.ts` should be replaced with the tables above. Keep the citation keys short (e.g. `strength.pelland2025`) and map each key to a numbered source here.
2. **Engine additions are needed** before about 20 templates can be verified automatically:
   - `sets_logged` (per muscle group, with a 0.5 multiplier for indirect sets as in Pelland [1])
   - a `self_report` criterion kind (lowest trust, capped XP)
   - an `improve_mean` criterion for weekly averages vs baseline
   - a `plyometric` session type
   - the metrics `alcohol_drinks_day`, `wake_deviation_min`, `vilpa_bouts_day` and `hr_time_above_85pct`
   - an `opt_in` tag (alcohol, creatine, sauna, bone) that the scheduler never assigns by default
3. **Copy rules follow from the evidence:**
   - Say "easy aerobic", not "mitochondrial Zone 2" [10].
   - Say "near failure", not "to failure" [2].
   - Never promise lifespan years; CRF and VILPA data are associations [15][16][29].
   - Show the tier badge on every quest, as the core principle requires.
4. **Anti-hype positioning gets concrete counter-examples.** The "Top 0.1 %" stack's water, NAC, megadose vitamin D, high-dose melatonin and Zone-2-only claims can each be answered with a cited "what the evidence actually says" card [10][39][42][43].
5. **No-punishment design is consistent with the data.** Missed weeks cost little physiologically: a one-week full break did not reduce hypertrophy [6]. The UI can truthfully say "a missed week barely moves the needle" (confidence: medium).
6. **Proof claims should use coarse, verifiable thresholds**, for example "150+ active minutes for 4 weeks, device-verified" or "7k-step average, device-verified". Fine-grained HRV or sleep-score claims carry the measurement error documented in wearable-integrations.md.

## Open questions

- Pelland's sample was 79 % male with a mean age of 25 [1]. How well the dose tables fit women and 50+ users is unknown. Should Tier I be larger for older users?
- The sources and exact cut-offs for AASM/SRS "≥7 h" and the SRI targets need primary-source verification before Sanctum goes live.
- The Helgerud and Billat effect sizes should be checked against full texts [12][14].
- How should "sedentary breaks" be derived consistently across Apple stand hours, Garmin move bar and Health Connect (no common data type verified)?
- Do any quests (bone-loading, plyometrics for 60+, alcohol reduction) trigger medical-device or health-claim issues? Cross-check with regulatory.md.
- Should a pre-participation screen (e.g. PAR-Q+) gate the Engine III and Arena tiers? Not researched here.
- Nielsen-type "weekly running distance increase" data were not verified (search budget exhausted).
